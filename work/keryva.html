Case study 02 / Ministry technology

Keryva

A scripture-grounded companion for pastors and ministry leaders: sermon preparation, study guides, prayer journaling, fasting plans, and Sunday service planning, built offline-first and available in six languages including Yoruba, Igbo, and Nigerian Pidgin.

01 / The problem

A pastor preparing a Sunday sermon is usually doing five jobs at once: studying the text, writing the message, preparing a bulletin, answering prayer requests, and posting to the church's social pages, most of it late at night with no assistant and no research budget. Existing Bible software reads scripture well but does nothing with it. General AI tools write fast but hallucinate scripture references with total confidence, which is a serious problem when the output is going to be preached from a pulpit.

The real risk with AI and scripture isn't that it's unhelpful. It's that it's convincing and wrong, and nobody double-checks a verse reference before Sunday.

02 / The thinking

I considered building a single AI sermon generator and stopping there. I rejected it because a sermon is the smallest part of a pastor's actual week; the study guide, the Sunday bulletin, the prayer desk, and the social content all draw from the same source material and none of the tools I looked at connected them. So the real product wasn't "AI writes your sermon," it was one workspace where a single scripture theme could become a sermon, a study guide, a bulletin, and a social post without retyping it four times.

The harder decision was translation. Yoruba, Igbo, and Pidgin are underserved in Bible software, and the honest answer for several of them is that no authorised translation exists for every book. I rejected silently falling back to machine translation. Every machine-translated passage is labelled as such in the interface, because a congregation deserves to know the difference between an authorised text and a best-effort translation.

Sermon Studio

From a topic to a preachable draft, with a way to check every reference.

A pastor sets topic, audience, and tone. The draft comes back with scripture woven in, each reference tappable to jump straight to the verse in their chosen translation. Preach Mode turns the finished draft into a full-screen teleprompter with a timer, so the last step before Sunday is rehearsal, not formatting.

03 / The business

Keryva is free, which was a deliberate call, not an oversight. The people it serves best, small church leaders and volunteer study group leads across Nigeria and the diaspora, are the least likely to pay a SaaS subscription for a tool this specific, and a paywall would have killed adoption before it started. The business case here isn't subscription revenue; it's a live, complex, multilingual AI product I can point to when I talk to clients about what I can build.

04 / The users and constraints

Internet access across the user base is inconsistent, so offline was a requirement from day one, not a stretch goal: cached chapters, localStorage, and IndexedDB mean a pastor can keep working through a study guide with no signal. That constraint shaped the whole architecture before a single screen was designed, which is also why Keryva runs as a Vite-built PWA instead of a heavier framework: less to download, faster to install, and it behaves like a native app once it's on the home screen.

Running four AI providers (Gemini, Claude, Groq, OpenRouter) in a fallback chain was a direct response to one constraint: this app cannot go down on a Saturday night while someone is finishing tomorrow's sermon. If one provider is rate-limited or down, the next takes over automatically.

05 / The outcome

Keryva is live at v2.4.0 with 25 plus pages covering the full ministry workflow, from Bible reading through sermon delivery through social content. I don't have adoption analytics to quote yet, since accounts and cloud sync are still on the roadmap, and I'd rather say that plainly than invent a number. What I can point to is scope and reliability: a product with a four-provider AI fallback chain and honest, labelled translation has not gone down on a pastor mid-sermon since it shipped.
